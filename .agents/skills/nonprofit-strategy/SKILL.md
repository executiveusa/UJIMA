# Nonprofit Strategy

## Purpose

Help agents reason about nonprofit organizations as mission-driven operating systems with multiple stakeholder groups, constrained capacity, public trust obligations, and a need for measurable impact plus financial sustainability.

## Use when

Use for nonprofit positioning, program strategy, fundraising support, donor communications, community partnerships, grant-readiness planning, volunteer systems, board/stakeholder communications, impact storytelling, and capacity planning.

## Required intake

Before strategy, resolve:

- mission and population served;
- geography and cultural context;
- current programs;
- evidence of demand;
- beneficiaries and consent considerations;
- funders/donors/sponsors;
- board/governance constraints;
- staff and volunteer capacity;
- current revenue mix;
- known compliance or reporting obligations;
- current paid/approved project scope.

Unknowns remain unknown. Do not invent impact metrics, community need, testimonials, partnerships, demographic claims, or beneficiary stories.

## Stakeholder map

Evaluate every major decision against:

1. people served;
2. families/community;
3. staff/volunteers;
4. board/governance;
5. donors/funders/sponsors;
6. partners/vendors;
7. public/community trust.

A strategy that wins one stakeholder group by harming another requires explicit review.

## Program test

For each program or campaign, capture:

- problem/job to be done;
- target participant;
- desired change;
- delivery mechanism;
- capacity limit;
- cost drivers;
- risks;
- leading indicators;
- outcome evidence;
- story/proof collection method;
- next funding or sustainability step.

## Ethical storytelling law

Never use hardship, trauma, poverty, disability, youth vulnerability, immigration status, family instability, or identity as spectacle.

Prefer:

- agency over pity;
- systems/context over savior narratives;
- consent over convenience;
- real proof over emotional inflation;
- dignity over shock value;
- community voice over organizational self-congratulation.

## Offer design for nonprofits

A strong offer should make the exchange clear:

- what participation/support enables;
- who benefits;
- what the organization actually does;
- what proof exists;
- what action is being requested;
- what happens next.

Avoid vague `support our mission` language when a concrete action can be named.

## Fundraising boundary

Agents may prepare research, messaging, segmentation, draft materials, evidence packets, and campaign plans. Outbound donor contact, claims about deductibility, legal/tax representations, grant certifications, and commitments require the appropriate human approval and professional review where applicable.

## Capacity guardrail

Do not market demand beyond delivery capacity without a queue, limit, waitlist, eligibility rule, or transparent first-come policy.

## Success review

Ask:

- Did mission impact improve?
- Can the organization deliver what it promised?
- Is the evidence credible?
- Did communication preserve dignity?
- Did the work create reusable organizational capacity?
- Is the next action financially and operationally sustainable?
