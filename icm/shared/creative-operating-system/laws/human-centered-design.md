# Human-Centered Design Laws

## Sources

- IDEO Design Kit: https://www.designkit.org/human-centered-design.html
- IDEO Field Guide: https://www.designkit.org/resources/1.html
- Apple design principles: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/design-principles
- Nielsen Norman Group empathy mapping: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empathy-mapping/

## Law 1 — Begin with people, not assumptions

Before proposing a solution, identify the people affected, the task they are trying to complete, the environment in which they act, the barriers they face, and what success means to them.

Do not treat internal stakeholder preference as user evidence.

## Law 2 — Learn directly

Use interviews, observation, existing behavior, support questions, event participation, and tested prototypes. Record what people do as well as what they say.

## Law 3 — Include those most affected

For social-purpose work, include youth, families, volunteers, staff, partners, and accessibility needs at the appropriate level. Never assume leadership alone can represent every participant.

## Law 4 — Separate evidence from interpretation

Discovery output must distinguish:

- direct quote;
- observed behavior;
- verified fact;
- team interpretation;
- unresolved assumption.

## Law 5 — Define the real problem

A problem statement must describe the person, context, barrier, and desired change without prescribing the solution.

Bad:
> We need a mobile app.

Better:
> Parents need a fast, trustworthy way to confirm event eligibility and register from a phone without creating a new account.

## Law 6 — Generate alternatives before committing

Consider multiple ways to achieve the objective. Prefer the smallest approach that can be tested with real people and that preserves safety, ownership, and reversibility.

## Law 7 — Prototype the risky assumption

Prototype the part most likely to fail: message clarity, form completion, trust, logistics, eligibility, consent, or handoff. Do not spend production effort polishing an untested premise.

## Law 8 — Test behavior, not taste

Ask participants to complete a realistic task. Observe confusion, hesitation, errors, abandonment, and recovery. Do not rely only on whether someone says they like the design.

## Law 9 — Close the feedback loop

Document what changed because of participant input. When feedback is not adopted, record the reason, especially when safety, law, feasibility, or conflicting needs are involved.

## Law 10 — Design with, not merely for

When the work represents a community's identity or lived experience, create meaningful opportunities for review and correction before publication.

## Discovery checklist

- Who is most affected?
- Who is missing from the conversation?
- What evidence do we have?
- What remains an assumption?
- What task are people trying to complete?
- What creates friction or mistrust?
- What is the smallest testable solution?
- Which assumption is riskiest?
- How will we observe real behavior?
- How will feedback change the source system?
