# Design, UX, and Brand Laws

## Law 1 — Purpose before decoration

Every screen, page, flyer, or asset must have one explicit job. Decorative elements are allowed only when they improve comprehension, emotional fit, recognition, or trust.

### Agent test

State the artifact's job in one sentence. If the design cannot be evaluated against that sentence, the brief is incomplete.

## Law 2 — One dominant action

Every conversion surface must present one primary action. Secondary actions must be visually subordinate and must not compete with the main task.

### Failure patterns

- multiple equally prominent buttons;
- donation, volunteer, registration, and newsletter actions competing above the fold;
- decorative animation pulling attention away from the task;
- vague labels such as `Explore`, `Learn`, or `Get involved` when a more specific verb exists.

## Law 3 — Self-evident before clever

A first-time user should understand where they are, what the artifact is, and what to do next without instructions. Use familiar patterns and plain labels. Clarity outranks novelty and internal terminology.

## Law 4 — Design for scanning

Assume people scan before they read.

Required:

- descriptive headings;
- short paragraphs;
- meaningful grouping;
- clear visual hierarchy;
- prominent dates, eligibility, location, and CTA;
- obvious clickable states;
- concise copy.

## Law 5 — Preserve agency and recovery

People must understand system status, be able to correct mistakes, and know what happens after an action. Forms must confirm success, explain errors, preserve entered data when possible, and never create false urgency.

## Law 6 — Accessibility is a starting constraint

Design mobile-first and support keyboard use, readable contrast, semantic headings, text alternatives, captions, reduced motion, plain-language validation, and sufficient target sizes. Never trade accessibility for visual style.

## Law 7 — Brand is promise plus proof

A brand is not a logo treatment. It is the accumulated expectation created by language, behavior, visual identity, service experience, and evidence.

Every artifact must align:

- promise;
- behavior;
- voice;
- visual system;
- proof;
- audience expectation.

## Law 8 — Coherence beats novelty

Do not introduce a new visual language for each campaign. Extend the tenant's established design tokens, typography, image treatment, spacing, and voice unless a documented reason requires change.

## Law 9 — Restraint is active editing

When uncertain, remove. White space, pacing, silence, and limited emphasis are production tools. Everything cannot be important.

## Law 10 — Test with real people

High-confidence design still requires observation. For critical pages, test the main task with at least three representative users or staff members. Record where they hesitate, misread, or abandon.

## Approval checklist

- Can a first-time visitor identify the page purpose in ten seconds?
- Is there one dominant action?
- Are essential facts visible without searching?
- Are labels literal and action-oriented?
- Is system status visible?
- Can mistakes be corrected?
- Does the artifact meet accessibility basics?
- Does it reinforce the tenant's promise and proof?
- Has unnecessary copy or decoration been removed?
- Is the output original rather than an imitation of a reference brand?
